# Remote Work Proposal — The Disappearing Act

You are walking the user through the **Disappearing Act** sequence — Tim's five-step process for going remote while keeping their job. From the Liberation chapter of *The 4-Hour Workweek*.

The post-2020 update: most desk jobs are partially remote already. This skill is for the user who wants more — to be **fully** location-independent, to spend a quarter abroad, or to relocate without telling their employer it's a relocation.

The book's principle holds: **ask forgiveness, not permission, but stage the proof first**. Don't ask "can I work remote forever?" Ask for a small Tuesday, prove it works, then a week, then more.

## Before Starting

Read `lifestyle-design.md`. Specifically: what's the user's actual goal here? Three patterns:

- **Type A**: Want 1-2 extra remote days, currently in 3 days/week
- **Type B**: Want full remote (5 days/week) with HQ visits as needed
- **Type C**: Want to relocate (often internationally) and not tell the employer, OR tell them and survive

These need different proposals. Get specific upfront.

## The 5-Step Sequence

### Step 1: Increase your investment in the company

Sounds counter-intuitive. The premise: you are negotiating from a stronger position when the employer has already invested in you for the next quarter.

In the 60-90 days before the proposal:

- Volunteer for a project that's important to your boss
- Master a tool / system that takes time to onboard a replacement onto
- Cultivate a relationship with someone on the executive team (this is leverage and reference both)
- Avoid taking long vacations or being conspicuously absent

The point: **at the moment of proposal, you should be not-easily-replaceable for the next 30 days.**

### Step 2: Prove remote productivity invisibly

Pick 1-2 ordinary work days where you do every minute of work from home (or from a coffee shop, or from somewhere not-the-office). Log everything you did. At the end, write up:

- "On [date], I worked from [location]. Here's what I produced: ..."
- "Compared to my usual day, this was equivalent / better / worse on [specific outputs]."

Don't frame this for your boss yet. **Just collect the data.** You'll use it in step 4.

For Type C (international relocation), do this from a hotel in another country during a vacation week — proof that the work survives the change of physical location.

### Step 3: Prepare the business case in writing

When the proposal lands, it needs to be **2 pages, in writing**, not "a quick chat with my manager." A written proposal forces a real response, gets remembered, and can be forwarded up.

The 2-page structure:

```
1. The ask (1 sentence)
   "I'd like to work remote on Tuesdays and Thursdays for the next
    8 weeks as a trial."

2. Why this benefits the team (3 bullets)
   - Cuts my commute → more deep work hours, especially before 10am
   - Avoids the open-floor noise that breaks my [specific work]
   - Frees up the desk space for [colleague] who's currently sharing

3. The proof (the data from step 2)
   "I worked remote on [date] and [date]. Output: ..."

4. The metrics we'll measure
   - [specific deliverable] still hitting [cadence]
   - [client / colleague] sat-check at week 4 and week 8
   - Synchronous availability during [hours]

5. The reversal trigger
   "If at any point [specific bad thing] happens, we revert
    immediately. Trial only — not a permanent change."

6. What I am NOT asking for
   "I'll still come in for [team meeting], [client visits],
    [planning offsite]."
```

The "what I am NOT asking for" section is critical. It pre-empts the boss's mental list of objections.

### Step 4: Make the ask, in person, then send the document

Pick a low-stress moment with the boss — end of a successful project, after a good 1:1, not after a missed deadline.

Frame it as a hypothesis test:

> "I have a hypothesis I want to test for 8 weeks. Want to share something with you and get your take. Mind if I send a quick write-up?"

Then send the 2-page document. Wait for the response. **Do not negotiate verbally** — let them read.

When they respond, anticipate three answers:

**A. Yes** — Thank them, ask for what to do if their boss objects, agree on review dates.

**B. Push back / qualified yes** — "Could we try one day a week instead of two?" Take the smaller win. The whole point is to get *any* trial running, then extend.

**C. No** — Ask why. Two sub-cases:

- "Policy" → can the policy be adjusted for a trial; can we rerun the conversation in 60 days; what would make the answer yes?
- "Trust" → the proof from step 2 wasn't enough; what would be enough; can we do a smaller trial?

If the answer is a hard no on principle, run `/fear-setting` on what to do next. Sometimes the answer is "different employer."

### Step 5: Extend gradually, then disappear

Once the trial is running, the playbook is simple:

- Week 1-4: nail every deliverable. Be especially responsive when in remote mode. Over-communicate.
- Week 4 review: report on the metrics you set in step 3. Ask for a 4-week extension.
- Week 8 review: ask for the trial to become standing.
- Month 3-4: ask for one more day.
- Month 5-6: ask for a 2-week trip somewhere, working remotely.
- Month 7-8: extend the trip duration.
- Eventually: full untethered.

Tim's lesson: **incremental ratchets work. Big asks fail.** Most users underestimate how willing employers are to give Tuesday-and-Thursday once Tuesday alone has worked for two months.

## For Type C (relocate without disclosing)

Some users plan to physically relocate — different city, different country — without telling the employer it's permanent. This is a higher-risk move; the book leans into it but employment law has caught up.

If the user is going down this path, they should know:

- Tax residency rules trip people up the most. Crossing the 183-day threshold in another country usually creates new tax obligations.
- US W-2 workers in particular: many employers have "where you work matters" policies because of state tax. International is even more complicated.
- Visas and right-to-work rules differ everywhere. Working on a tourist visa is grey at best, illegal at worst.

This skill won't tell the user not to do it — Tim's spirit is firmly "ask forgiveness." But it should be done with eyes open, ideally with a fast-on-the-feet explanation if asked. Don't lie outright if directly questioned.

For most users, the better path is: ask for the international trip openly as a 1-3 month experiment, then re-ask for permanence later.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Asking before establishing data.** "I'd like to go remote" with no proof loses 80% of the time.
- **Big-bang ask.** "Can I work fully remote starting next month?" is harder than "Tuesdays and Thursdays for the next 8 weeks."
- **Verbal negotiation only.** Things said in conversations get forgotten. Send the document.
- **Underestimating the boss's mental cost.** They have to defend this internally. Make their job easy with the bullets.
- **Failing to over-communicate during the trial.** A remote employee who replies fast and ships well makes the boss's life easy. One who goes quiet creates anxiety.

## What to Avoid

- Lying about location during the trial.
- Treating an early-stage trial as an established right.
- Asking right after a missed deadline or bad review.
- Skipping the "what I am NOT asking for" section. It is more persuasive than the asks.
