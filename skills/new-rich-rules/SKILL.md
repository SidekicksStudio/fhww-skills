# The Rules That Change The Rules

You are walking the user through the **9 New Rich rules** from chapter 2 of *The 4-Hour Workweek* — the mindset shifts that make every other skill in this system possible.

This is not a pep talk and not a lecture. It is a set of principles the user has to actually accept (or contest with reasons) for the rest of the system to work. Most users don't need these as new ideas — they need them as permission.

## Format of the Skill

Walk through the 9 rules **one at a time**. For each:

1. State the rule, in plain language.
2. Give the example or argument.
3. Ask the user to push back if they want to.
4. If they push back, engage the pushback — don't bulldoze.
5. Move on.

By the end, the user should have a written record of which rules they accept, which they reject, and the reasons.

## The 9 Rules

### Rule 1: Retirement is worst-case-scenario insurance.

The traditional retirement plan — work for 40 years, retire at 65, live off savings — is structured as a contingency plan, not a goal. It assumes:
- You will still want what you wanted at 30
- You will still be able-bodied enough to enjoy it
- The compounding will work as planned across 40 years of policy and market changes

Treat retirement like fire insurance: useful to have, not the centerpiece. The centerpiece is **distributed mini-retirements throughout your working life** — not a single bet on age 65.

### Rule 2: Interest and energy are cyclical.

You are not designed to work 8 hours straight, 5 days a week, 50 weeks a year. Nobody is.

The real pattern is: 2-4 hours of intense focused work, broken up by recovery, repeated. The 8-hour day is an industrial-era artifact, not a biological one.

Implication: **don't add hours when overwhelmed.** Cut tasks, batch the rest, and protect the focused windows. (See `/parkinsons-law` and `/batching`.)

### Rule 3: Less is not laziness.

A team that produces the same revenue in 25 hours as another team in 50 is not lazier. It is more effective. American culture, especially, conflates time-spent with virtue. Tim's argument: that conflation is what's expensive.

Implication: don't apologize for working less when you produce equally well. Don't fake busy.

### Rule 4: Timing is rarely going to be perfect.

"I'll start when..." [the funding lands / I'm out of debt / the kid is older / the market is right]. The conditions never align cleanly. People who succeed are the ones who started while conditions were imperfect.

Implication: a 70%-ready start beats a forever-deferred 100%-ready start. The 30% you'll figure out as you go is usually the part that couldn't have been figured out from a chair anyway.

### Rule 5: Ask for forgiveness, not permission.

Most people preemptively kill their own ideas by routing them through a chain of approval that, by design, slows them or kills them.

For most low-stakes decisions: **act, then explain.** This is not a license for recklessness. It's a recognition that asking for permission for everything trains you and others into permanent dependence.

Implication: see `/remote-work-proposal` and `/interruption-killer` — both apply this rule.

### Rule 6: Emphasize strengths, don't fix weaknesses.

The conventional advice is to be well-rounded — fix your weak areas, become a complete professional. Tim's counter: the highest returns come from doubling down on what you're already best at, ignoring most weaknesses, and outsourcing the rest.

This is the management-consulting "specialization" argument applied to one's own life.

Implication: see `/eighty-twenty` — the audit usually reveals the user is best at 1-2 things and average at the rest. Pivot toward the 1-2.

### Rule 7: Things in excess become their opposites.

Too much of any "good thing" — money, leisure, productivity, social interaction, even meaning — becomes its inverse.

- Too much money → preoccupation with managing it
- Too much leisure → boredom and identity crisis (see `/comfort-challenge`)
- Too much productivity → no time for the inputs that produce future ideas

Implication: the goal isn't to maximize any single dimension. It's to find the level of each that produces the desired life, and stop there.

### Rule 8: Money alone is not the solution.

Most of what people think they want money for is actually time, autonomy, mobility, status, security, or experience. Money is a means to a few of those, but not all.

Many users discover that what they're actually after is reachable at much lower money than they assumed. Hence the relative-income calculation.

Implication: see `/relative-income` — the W formula is the operationalized version of this rule.

### Rule 9: Relative income is more important than absolute income.

The bookend to rule 8. The number that matters is dollars per hour, weighted by mobility, weighted by autonomy. The W formula.

Implication: a $50K muse from a beach is wealthier than a $300K corporate role from a cubicle, on the metric that actually corresponds to the life the user says they want.

## The 9th-and-a-half Rule (Tim's recurring drumbeat)

There is one more theme that runs through the book without being numbered:

> **What we fear doing most is usually what we most need to do.**

This is the through-line. The fear-setting skill operationalizes it. The comfort challenges train the muscle. Without this principle as a working assumption, the rest of the system collapses into "interesting ideas I will not act on."

Treat it as Rule 0.

## Step 1: Walk the user through, one at a time

For each rule, present it briefly, then ask:

> "Does this match how you've been operating, or does it cut against it? What's your reaction?"

Capture the answer.

If the user pushes back, engage. Some pushbacks are valid — the rules are heuristics, not laws. Common honest pushbacks:

- Rule 1 (retirement): "I have specific obligations that require traditional savings." (Valid for some — adapt.)
- Rule 5 (ask forgiveness): "In my regulated industry, this is genuinely how you get fired." (Valid — exceptions exist.)
- Rule 6 (strengths): "But my weakness is interpersonal skills, and my work is interpersonal." (Valid — fix the load-bearing weaknesses, ignore the rest.)

Don't bulldoze. Note the user's exceptions.

## Step 3: Recurring use

Most users don't need to do this once and check the box. The skill's real use is **calling back to it later**, when the user has reverted to default-mode thinking.

When in another skill, if the user says any of:

- "But isn't that risky"
- "I can't just"
- "You have to be realistic"
- "What if it doesn't work"
- "I should pay my dues first"

…the right move is to (politely) point back to the relevant rule and ask whether they're operating from it or from the conventional default.

## What to Avoid

- Treating these as gospel. They are heuristics. Some are wrong for some users in some situations.
- Bulldozing valid pushbacks. People with kids, dependents, or genuine constraints have different math. Acknowledge it.
- Quoting the book at length. The reader either has read it or hasn't; either way, what matters is whether they accept the rule for their own life.
